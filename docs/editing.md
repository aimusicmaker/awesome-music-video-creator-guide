# Edit for the song

[Home](../README.md) · [First video](first-video.md) · [Shot template](../starter-kit/brief-template.md)

## Plan around changes you can hear

Import the song first. Place timeline markers at the start of the phrase, the first clear drum hit, a chorus entrance, and the end of the phrase. Choose one moment to emphasize. If every cut is equally dramatic, the important moment disappears.

For steady music, a useful estimate is `seconds per beat = 60 / BPM`. At 120 BPM, four beats last two seconds. For live performances, tempo changes, pickup notes, or uncertain BPM, trust listening and the waveform. Cut at the audible event, not simply at the nearest grid line.

## Give every shot one job

A wide shot establishes a place. A detail shot provides texture. A portrait connects the viewer to a person. A reveal changes the meaning of what came before. Alternate jobs rather than switching to an unrelated scene every few seconds.

For a full song, draw an energy map before generating: quiet verse, rising pre-chorus, open chorus, reduced bridge, final return. Reuse visual motifs with changed scale or lighting. [Recipe 12](../prompts/12-full-song.md) turns that idea into a shot plan.

## Keep identity stable

Make one reference sheet with an approved face, wardrobe, prop, palette, and location. Use that reference each time the tool supports it. Maintain screen direction: a person walking left-to-right should not suddenly reverse unless the edit explains it. If hands, instruments, or lip movements break, use a shorter shot or cut to a detail without a visible mouth.

## Put typography in the editor

Generate the image without lyrics. Add exact text afterward, using one or two lines at a time and enough contrast to read it over the background. Try a solid or translucent background panel if the scene is busy. Match a lyric’s appearance to the audible phrase; do not rely on auto-transcription for names or unusual words. Watch the video at phone size and correct every line.

## Make a loop that survives repetition

A locked camera and small repeating motions are easier to join than a continuous push-in. Put two copies of the clip together and inspect the seam. Try trimming to similar opening/closing frames or adding a short dissolve, then check for doubled shapes. A reverse duplicate works for abstract movement but can make rain rise and people walk backward. Loop the image separately from the song; do not cut off an unfinished musical phrase just to fit a visual cycle.

## Export a draft before the final version

| Decision | Practical starting point |
|---|---|
| Vertical social edit | 9:16; keep the subject and text comfortably inside the frame |
| Landscape music video | 16:9; check portrait crops separately |
| Square cover motion | 1:1; preserve the artwork’s main subject |
| File format | MP4, H.264 video, AAC audio if available |
| Resolution | Match the useful source detail; inspect any upscale |
| Frame rate | Keep the source rate unless there is a clear reason to convert |
| Sound | Use one chosen song track; mute generated audio unless intentionally mixed |

These are editing defaults, not official platform upload limits. Use the destination’s current upload preview to check cropping, overlays, captions, and any disclosure fields.

## Final playback checklist

- The first frame communicates a scene or idea immediately.
- Cuts follow the chosen musical events; no accidental black gaps appear.
- Faces, clothing, props, and screen direction remain understandable.
- Lyrics and titles are spelled correctly and readable on a phone.
- Audio does not double, clip, or cut off unexpectedly.
- The exported file plays from beginning to end outside the editor.
- Your track, images, likenesses, and any third-party material are cleared for the intended use.
